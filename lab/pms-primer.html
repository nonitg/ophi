Reading the Chart

What a dental practice management system actually is, taught through the one on our lab machine — ABELDent 15.1, its Fictional Data set, and the four subsystems every PMS turns out to be.

Orientation

A PMS is four systems wearing one coat

A practice management system looks like a single application and is not. It is a schedule, a clinical chart, a money ledger, and an insurance module, joined by nothing more than a shared patient ID. Each was built by different people in a different decade, and each stores its data in a shape that made sense to that subsystem alone.

That seam is the entire reason Ophi exists. A preauthorization needs evidence from three of the four at once, in three different formats, and a human currently does that join by opening four screens and comparing dates by eye.

Everything below is measured from the real install, not from the manual. Where a table name appears in the margin, that is where the concept actually lives on disk.

Subsystem one · the scheduleapt

Chairs multiplied by time

The front desk's world. An appointment carries a patient, a provider, a column (the operatory — the physical chair), a start time, and a duration counted in units rather than minutes. Ten to fifteen minutes per unit, set per practice.

An appointment also carries Work to do — a free-text line like consult. That is a plan expressed as a string, not a link to a procedure record. The schedule knows what is supposed to happen. It does not know what the chart says, and nothing reconciles the two.

Subsystem two · the clinical chartPerioChartsNotes

Three stores people call one thing

"The chart" is shorthand for three unrelated data structures.

The odontogram

Per-tooth, per-surface state: what is present, what is restored, what is missing. A tooth has five named surfaces, so a restoration is identified as a tooth plus a surface combination — #46 MO is the mesial and occlusal surfaces of the lower right first molar.

The periodontal chart

Six pocket-depth measurements per tooth, taken with a graduated probe, plus recession, bleeding, suppuration, mobility and furcation. Thirty-two teeth times six sites is 192 measurements for one full-mouth exam. A hygienist calls them out and someone types them in.

ABELDent stores this exam not as 192 rows but as a fixed-width binary column: Pocket char(192), one byte per site, the value being the depth in millimetres, with 0xFF for a site that has no reading. Here is one real exam from the Fictional Data set, decoded:

Periodontal exam · patient 155 · exam 1 of 3 · 2004-12-13 Perio.Pocket → varbinary(192)
1–4 mm, healthy 5 mm 6 mm and deeper no reading — tooth absent or not probed

Read it as a clinician would: each column is a tooth, each cell a probe site. The hatched columns are teeth that are gone — and the pattern of which ones are gone is itself diagnostic. Here, eight are missing, including all four third molars. Three sites reach 5–7 mm, which is the depth at which a pocket stops being a crevice and starts being a disease.

Why the count matters more than the depths

A full-mouth exam of 192 points and a twelve-point spot check are opposite clinical statements, and nothing but the count separates them. A rule that asks "is there periodontal charting?" gets yes from both. A rule that asks "how many sites were recorded, and when?" does not. Our extractor reports the point count as a first-class field for exactly this reason.

Clinical notes

Free text, sometimes templated, carrying flags for deleted, superseded and signed-off. This is where the reason for a crown actually lives — in prose, written for another clinician, never for a payer. It is the least structured and most load-bearing part of the record.

Subsystem three · the ledgertdi

Every procedure has two lives

A procedure exists first as a plan — proposed, unbilled, no money moved — and later as a completed entry. Both live in the same table, in the same shape, separated by one flag.

Reading a planned crown as a completed one is the classic PMS integration bug. It is a single character, it is undocumented, and two of its four values are still unidentified on our own lab machine. We will not guess them — we will change something in the application and watch which rows move.

A ledger row also carries both a Provider and a Responsible Provider: who performed the work, and who owns the patient. On a preauthorization those are frequently different people, and only one of them is the treating dentist of record.

Subsystem four · insuranceixiinsnsj

A claim asks to be paid. A predetermination asks permission.

A patient holds one or more coverages: a carrier, a policy, a group number, a certificate number, and a relationship to whoever actually holds the plan — often a parent or a spouse, who may not be a patient of the practice at all.

Two transactions run over the same wire in the same format. A claim says this was done, pay me. A predetermination says this is proposed, will you cover it? — same message, different transaction code, no money. In the industry the words preauthorization, predetermination and pre-estimate are used interchangeably; they are the same submission.

Ophi operates only on the second. We assemble the packet. A human at the practice submits it, because the rules governing that wire require the treating dentist to be the sender of record.

The trapnotation

Two numbering systems, one database

Teeth are numbered, and the world does not agree on how. Canada uses FDI two-digit notation: first digit the quadrant, second the position from the midline, so 46 is the lower-right first molar. North American software frequently uses Universal numbering, a simple 1–32 sweep starting at the upper right third molar.

Both are in ABELDent, in different subsystems. The ledger records teeth in FDI. The periodontal arrays are indexed in Universal order. Joining a planned crown to the perio evidence for that same tooth requires a conversion that nothing in the database tells you is necessary.

The failure this produces

Tooth 36 in FDI is Universal index 19. Read the perio array at index 36 instead and you are either out of bounds or reading a different tooth's measurements — and in neither case does anything throw. You get a confident, wrong answer about a tooth the patient may not even have. This is why the conversion belongs in one tested function and never at a call site.

Six columns, four subsystems, one patient per row. Assembling that by hand for one case is a ten-minute job across four screens; the practice does it while the patient is still in the chair. Assembling it wrongly means a denial, a resubmission, and a patient who was told something that turned out not to be true.

Note the last column. Every radiograph table in this database is empty. That is the finding that most shapes the build — and it forces a distinction we have to carry in the type system, not in a comment:

Two sentences that must never collapse into one

"This patient has no radiograph of #36."
"This software cannot see radiographs."

The first is a documentation gap the practice can fix. The second is our limitation, and reporting it as the first would fabricate a gap that does not exist. Every section of every bundle we produce carries an assurance flag saying which of the two it is.

The limitno table holds this

The measurements that exist in no chart

The documentation criteria for a crown include the crown-to-root ratio, the absence of furcation involvement, the distance from the restoration margin to the alveolar crest, and the height of the ferrule. Four measurements, all of them radiographic.

None of them is a field in ABELDent. None of them is a field in any practice management system, because a dentist makes them by looking at the film. No amount of reading the database produces them, and extracting them from the image is a different product.

So we do not try. The packet renders them as attributed clinical judgment — the dentist confirms each criterion, and the document says who assessed what, on which tooth, on which date. That single decision removes image analysis from scope, puts the clinical assertion in the hands of the person legally responsible for it, and produces a stronger audit record than a generated paragraph ever would.

The line we hold everywhere

Ophi never says approved, eligible, or covered. It says: the criteria require a periapical within twelve months; the most recent periapical of #46 is dated 2023-11-14. Every statement is about documentation completeness against a cited rule — never about what a payer will do.
